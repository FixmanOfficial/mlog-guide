`rand` gives a random number **from zero up to but not including the one you give**, and that
number is fractional. A die comes out of it in two lines: first the throw, then rounding down.

<Example scene={RANDOM} />

`op rand throw 6` gives a fractional number below six — say 2.09; `floor` cuts the fraction and
leaves 2; add one and you get 1 to 6, like a real die. Press "step" a few times: the throw is
different every time.

The order matters here. Round `rand 5` first and then add, and a six never comes up at all;
take `rand 7` and round it, and a seven turns up now and then. An N-sided die is always
`rand N`, `floor`, `add 1`.

<Card kind="note">
Every `rand` is a new throw. Two lines in a row with `op rand a 10` and `op rand b 10` give
**different** numbers, not the same one: `rand` is not a property you ask for but an action.
</Card>

## In the example the numbers are always the same

Press "restart" and run the example again — the same thing comes up. The game is not like that:
there the generator is seeded randomly at start-up, and the same program gives different
numbers every time.

Here the seed is fixed on purpose: the lesson promises numbers, the tests check them, and an
example that shows something different every time can teach nothing. Keep that in mind when you
move a program into the game: there will be no repeats there.

## Noise is not randomness

<Example scene={NOISE} />

`noise` takes two coordinates and gives a number roughly between −1 and 1. It is **not
random**: the same point always has the same answer, and neighbouring points have nearly the
same one.

In the example the points (10, 10) and (10.01, 10) gave −0.524 and −0.520 — neighbours, and
their answers are close together. (40, 10) is far away, and the answer there is quite
different: 0.908.

That is exactly what makes noise useful. A random number jumps about, and anything built on it
flickers; noise changes smoothly, so hills, ore patches and stains on a map come out of it —
everything that should look uneven but not torn. The game draws terrain with it, and in logic
it is handy for a smoothly wandering value: a speed, a delay, a brightness.

<Card kind="recap">
- `rand N` is a fractional number from zero up to but not including N.
- A die is `rand N`, then `floor`, then `add 1`.
- Every `rand` throws afresh.
- `noise x y` is not randomness: a point always has one answer, and nearby points have close ones.
- In the course examples the seed is fixed; in the game it is random.
</Card>

<Card kind="task">
Make a coin: `op rand r 2` and `op floor heads r`. You get a zero or a one, and both sides come
up equally often.

Then replace `floor` with `round` and see what happens. There are now **three** outcomes: zero,
one and two, and the one comes up twice as often as the others. Rounding to nearest gathers the
whole stretch from 0.5 to 1.5 into the one, and only half a stretch each into zero and two.

A fair coin can be made with `round` as well, but from a different throw: `op rand r 1` and
`op round heads r`. The general rule: **`rand N` is rounded down, not to nearest**.
</Card>
