A packed colour is one number, and it cannot be read by eye. `unpackcolor` lays it back out
into four channels.

<Example scene={UNPACK} world={true} />

The program asks copper for its colour and prints it channel by channel: the board shows
`copper: 217 157 115`.

```
sensor colour @copper @color
unpackcolor r g b a colour
```

| Field | What is in it |
| --- | --- |
| the first four | where to put red, green, blue and alpha |
| fifth | the packed colour |

<Card kind="note">
The channels come back as **fractions from zero to one** — the same ones `packcolor` takes.
The familiar 0–255 are obtained by multiplying: `op mul r r 255`. In the example the
multiplication is followed by `op floor`, because dividing and multiplying by 255 leaves a tail
like `217.00000000000003`.
</Card>

## Why take a colour apart

Taking one apart is rarely needed for its own sake — colours are usually assembled rather than
read. There is one real use: **learning a colour somebody else chose**.

- an item's, liquid's or block's colour — `sensor @color`, and the display draws ore in its own
  colour;
- a team's colour — `sensor @color @crux`, so markers on the map match what the player sees
  around them;
- a colour that arrived from a memory cell or a neighbouring processor and needs a small
  change — dimming it by halving the channels and packing it back, for instance.

```
unpackcolor r g b a colour
op div r r 2
op div g g 2
op div b b 2
packcolor dim r g b a
```

That is the usual way of working with colour: take apart, adjust the channels, pack again.
The packed number itself must not be touched with arithmetic.

<Card kind="recap">
- `unpackcolor r g b a colour` lays a packed colour out into four channels.
- The channels are fractions from zero to one; 0–255 comes from multiplying.
- The main source of other people's colours is `sensor @color` on content and teams.
- To change a colour you take it apart, adjust the channels and pack it back with `packcolor`.
</Card>

<Card kind="task">
Change `@copper` in the example to `@graphite` and run it: the numbers come out different —
graphite is grey, and all three channels end up close to one another.

Then remove the three `op floor` lines and look at the board: instead of round numbers there
are tails. That is not a fault of the unpacking but the ordinary imprecision of fractional
numbers — there is <Lesson lesson="advanced/numbers">a lesson of its own</Lesson> about it.
</Card>
