You don't type a program in the game — you assemble it out of blocks. Click the processor,
a row of buttons appears under it, and the pencil in that row opens the program window.

Everything happens in that window from there on. It also stands on every page of this course:
the example below **is** that window, only with our own buttons on top.

<Example scene={EDITOR} world={false} />

## A line of a program

Every line is built the same way:

- a **header** — the instruction's name on the left, the line number on the right;
- **three buttons in the header**: the plus adds a new line **after this one**, the two pages
  copy the line, the cross deletes it;
- a **body** — the fields you write into. A field takes both a number and a variable name:
  the block does not care which, and in writing there is no difference between "a value"
  and "a variable".

The header colour is not decoration: it says which category the instruction comes from.
Input and output are greyish pink, block control is brick, operations are lilac, flow control
is turquoise, units are sandy, world ones are blue. The "Add…" menu uses the same colours,
and its sections are laid out by them.

Lines are reordered by dragging them by the header, and that is the first thing worth learning:
the order of the lines **is** the order of execution. "Move a line up" in mlog means "run it
earlier", not "make it more important".

## Tooltips on lines and fields

Instruction names and field labels have descriptions: hover the cursor or hold your finger,
and a black plate appears explaining what the instruction does and what goes in the field.
They work the same way on the course pages.

Not everything has one: signs like `<` and `>` have no tooltip in the game either — there is
nothing to explain there.

<Card kind="note">
These tooltips have no setting of their own: they are always on. The "Hints" setting in the
"Game" section has nothing to do with them — it removes the gameplay advice in the corner
of the screen and does not touch the program editor.
</Card>

## The buttons under the window

There are four buttons under the program window, and newcomers leave three of them unopened
for years.

- **"Back"** closes the window. The program saves itself; there is no separate "save" button.
- **"Edit…"** opens a small window with four actions: clear the program, copy it to the
  clipboard, paste from the clipboard and restart. It is the most useful button in the window —
  see the lesson <Lesson lesson="basics/text">"A program is text"</Lesson>.
- **"Variables"** shows what is in the processor's variables right now. It is the main
  debugging tool, and it gets the lesson <Lesson lesson="basics/watch">"The variables
  window"</Lesson>.
- **"Add…"** — a new line at the end of the program.

<Card kind="error">
Any edit to the program starts it over: the counter goes back to the first line and every
variable is cleared. You cannot append a line without losing what has been accumulated —
the game has no "continue from here".
</Card>

## Our buttons on top are not from the game

Above the example is a row the game does not have: run, step, "restart", add a line, the eye
and the book.

**Step** runs exactly one instruction. The game cannot do this: a processor is either running
or switched off. For learning it is the key button — you can watch one variable change at a time.

**"Restart"** does exist in the game, but it is hidden: it is "Restart" inside the "Edit…"
window.

**The eye** removes the frame around the next line: while the example is running it jumps
every tick, and that flickers.

**The book** switches the language of the labels — more on that below.

## Labels are translated, code is not

Since version 160 the game translates the labels inside the blocks: `Operation` becomes a word
in your own language, and so do `in` and `and`. The setting responsible is **"Logic
Localization"** in the "Graphics" section, and it is on by default.

The book button above the example does the same thing. Press it and look at the lines: names
and labels turn English, and the fields do not change by a single character.

That is the main thing to take away: **the translation does not touch the language**. In the
block's field, in the program's text and in a schematic it is still `sensor block1 @totalItems` —
in someone else's guide and in your own game alike, whatever the setting. Only the words around
the fields change.

<Card kind="error">
Hence a common confusion for newcomers: in a two-year-old video the block is called
`Operation`, and in your game it is a word in your own language — and it looks like two
different blocks, or two different versions of the language. It is the same block; flip the
book and compare.
</Card>

<Card kind="recap">
- The program opens by clicking the processor and then the pencil in the row of buttons.
- A line has a header with a name and a number, plus three buttons: add after, copy, delete.
- Lines are reordered by dragging the header — the order of lines is the order of execution.
- "Edit…" and "Variables" live under the window and get lessons of their own.
- Editing the program clears the variables and starts it from the first line.
- The translation changes the labels, not the code: the fields are always English.
</Card>

<Card kind="task">
Swap the second and third lines in the example by dragging one of them by the header, press
"restart" and walk through the first iteration a step at a time.

On the second step `total` becomes five, not twenty-five: the addition now comes before the
multiplication and adds to an empty variable. The program did not break and did not complain —
it does what is written, in the order it is written.

Take three more steps and `total` becomes twenty-five. The program reached the end and started
over, and by then `doubled` has already been computed, so the addition takes a ready number.
Why it loops is in the lesson [«How the processor works»](/en/course/basics/processor/); what
matters here is something else: a mistake in the order of lines is not always visible at once —
sometimes only on the first iteration.
</Card>
